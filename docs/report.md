**Status:** Version 2 educational prototype using fictional university policies

## Abstract

CampusCompass investigates whether a local retrieval augmented generation pipeline can provide useful university policy assistance while exposing its evidence and uncertainty. The system combines lexical or semantic retrieval, scope checks, evidence selection and optional instruction tuned language models. A controlled corpus contains 30 original fictional policies. Initial experiments showed that strong retrieval and exact source copying can coexist with irrelevant or incomplete answers. Version 2 therefore strengthens input validation, distinguishes semantic relevance from lexical overlap, assembles evidence for multiple question facets and exposes a decision trace. Evaluation separates original experiments, regression on previously inspected questions and a newly frozen audit set. Dense answerability accuracy improves from 47/69 to 56/69 on regression cases, but reaches only 25/40 on the new audit. Strictly verified local generation has low audit coverage. Citation membership and verbatim support are mechanical checks rather than semantic accuracy. The prototype demonstrates reproducible design analysis; institutional reliability and operational benefit require external annotation, realistic documents and a supervised pilot.

## 1 Problem definition and research objectives

A student asking “Can I still drop this class?” may need a deadline, a transcript consequence and a procedure for exceptional circumstances. These facts can be present on a university website yet difficult to locate without knowing formal administrative vocabulary. Repeated routine questions also consume staff time that could support unusual cases. CampusCompass addresses the language gap between student questions and policy documents. Its intended function is to explain general procedures and show supporting sources. Individual decisions about eligibility, appeals or exceptions remain with authorized staff.

Natural language processing is necessary because a useful response depends on both linguistic variation and policy distinctions. “I lost account access” can express the same need as “password recovery,” whereas withdrawing from one course differs materially from withdrawing from an institution. Keyword search returns documents without interpreting these relationships. A topic classifier can route a question but cannot explain conditions within a rule. An unrestricted chatbot can produce fluent guidance without knowing the applicable local policy. The selected design consequently combines retrieval with bounded response construction rather than treating fluency as evidence of correctness.

Retrieval augmented generation supplies external evidence to a language model. Lewis et al. identify attribution and knowledge updating as difficulties for purely parametric systems [1]. CampusCompass applies this separation through independently replaceable retrieval and generation components; it does not reproduce their jointly trained architecture. Updating a policy changes the evidence collection without requiring model retraining. A user can inspect the cited document rather than rely solely on the generator's wording. However, retrieval does not ensure that a passage answers the question, and a citation does not prove that the answer preserves every exception.

The research objectives are therefore to compare inexpensive lexical retrieval with pretrained semantic retrieval, investigate the relationship between evidence recall and answer acceptance, and test whether explicit evidence controls expose consequential failures. The central hypothesis is limited: semantic retrieval may recover paraphrased policy questions more effectively than lexical matching in this controlled collection. It is not assumed that adding a language model or combining retrievers automatically improves answer quality. The extractive route remains a necessary baseline against which generative complexity must justify itself.

The project uses a fictional institution to permit transparent development without disclosing student information or presenting invented rules as official advice. Every policy, amount, date and scenario is an educational fixture. This limits external validity but makes source attribution inspectable. Technical success concerns relevant evidence, faithful response construction and appropriate abstention. Service success concerns verified task completion and reduced effort; these benefits remain hypotheses because no student study or institutional deployment was conducted.

## 2 Data design and preprocessing

The corpus contains 30 original English policy passages spanning registration, finance, housing, library services, information technology and related campus processes. Each JSONL record includes an identifier, title, department, version, effective date and text. Complete policies are available to every retriever, while evaluation questions and annotations are stored separately. Expected answers are never inserted into the retrieval index. Stable identifiers connect source text, evidence panels and evaluation labels without inventing institutional URLs.

The original benchmark contains 106 questions divided into 37 development and 69 test cases. Development includes 30 single-policy questions, six unanswerables and one question requiring two policies. Test includes 60 single-policy paraphrases, six unanswerables and three questions requiring two policies, one containing a fictional email placeholder. Relevant identifiers specify the minimum evidence needed. Diagnostic topic terms are not exact reference answers. The same project authors created the policies and questions, so this split tests changed wording within a known collection rather than independent institutional generalization.

Version 2 is evaluated after the original test outputs were inspected. Those questions must therefore be treated as regression data: improvements can show that an identified defect was repaired, but cannot establish unseen performance. A further 40-question audit was authored by a separate evaluation agent and frozen before Version 2 outputs were collected. Its wording was withheld from the implementing agent until implementation freeze. It includes 28 supported and 12 unsupported cases, with eight two-policy questions and multilingual probes. This separation reduces direct implementation access, but remains internal project authorship and is not external or human-independent validation.

Ingestion validates the six-field schema, nonempty string values, identifier format, effective-date syntax and document-length limit. Duplicate identifiers and an empty corpus are rejected. These controls prevent malformed records from quietly contaminating attribution; they do not verify that a policy is institutionally correct. A real collection would additionally need publisher approval, source URLs, effective intervals and a mechanism for superseded documents. The supplied data card records provenance, fictional status, distribution conditions and limitations.

Lexical preprocessing lowercases alphanumeric tokens, removes a documented stop list and maps selected inflections and campus synonyms. The displayed source remains unchanged, preserving dates, negation and numerical thresholds. Such normalization supports matching rather than asserting semantic equivalence. Overaggressive stemming or synonym expansion can connect unrelated rules; for example, “leave” may indicate either absence from study or departure from housing. Each alias should therefore be reviewed as a maintainable engineering assumption.

Each short policy is indexed as one complete retrieval unit. An audit using the actual pinned MiniLM tokenizer found a maximum of 86 encoded tokens including title and text, below the implementation's 256-token limit; no supplied passage is clipped. This supports document-level indexing for these fixtures. Longer institutional documents would require heading-aware sections with stable parent identifiers and modest overlap. Chunking should preserve conditions and table associations. OCR confidence, duplicate content and conflicting versions would require ingestion review before such material could safely become answer evidence.

## 3 Pipeline architecture and model selection

The pipeline validates a question, screens scope and common sensitive patterns, retrieves evidence, assesses support, constructs a response and exposes its sources. The browser interface displays the retrieval mode, response status and selected evidence. Version 2 adds a stage-by-stage trace so a reviewer can distinguish a privacy redaction, scope refusal, insufficient relevance and successful evidence assembly. This design turns the demonstration into an inspectable experiment rather than a single opaque chatbot output.

![Figure 1 CampusCompass request processing and evidence controls](docs/figures/architecture.png)

BM25 provides a strong lexical baseline without downloading model weights. Its term-frequency saturation and document-length normalization follow the Okapi ranking family [2]. The implementation fixes k1 at 1.5 and b at 0.75 and duplicates title terms for weighting. A TF-IDF cosine baseline tests whether the particular probabilistic scoring rule materially changes retrieval. These settings are engineering choices, not learned parameters. Exact terms, policy names and administrative identifiers favor lexical methods, while colloquial paraphrases can lack sufficient word overlap.

Semantic retrieval uses the pretrained all-MiniLM-L6-v2 encoder, which produces 384-dimensional vectors [3]. The implementation applies attention-mask mean pooling and L2 normalization through Transformers and PyTorch. Normalized vector dot products represent cosine similarity. Exhaustive search over 30 policies is simpler and easier to reproduce than a vector database. The model is an English embedding choice, so successful multilingual examples would not justify general multilingual support. Dense Passage Retrieval illustrates learned dual-encoder retrieval [5]; CampusCompass uses a pretrained encoder and does not train a local dense retriever on this small corpus.

Two fusion variants combine rankings. Lexical fusion uses BM25 and TF-IDF; neural hybrid combines BM25 and MiniLM. Each sums reciprocal rank contributions, 1/(60 + rank), rather than treating incomparable raw scores as equivalent. Rank fusion can preserve complementary matches, but may also retain a confidently irrelevant lexical result. Its additional complexity is useful only if controlled evaluation supports it. The original experiments therefore retain all individual retrievers instead of reporting only the best-looking hybrid configuration.

A central Version 1 defect was using lexical token coverage as the answer gate even when retrieval used embeddings. A semantically relevant paraphrase could be rejected for sharing few words with the correct policy. Version 2 uses encoder cosine directly for neural modes and token coverage for lexical modes, with separately selected thresholds. Neither measure is a probability of correctness or an entailment judgment. A high cosine can still select a neighboring policy, and a threshold cannot verify that all requested details are present.

Version 2 also separates a question into a small number of conjunction-based facets when each segment contains useful content. It retrieves evidence per facet and requires every facet to exceed the configured support threshold. The deterministic response returns complete selected policy paragraphs, preserving nearby exceptions instead of selecting three potentially incomplete sentences from one source. This improves inspectability for multiple-policy questions, but the lightweight parser can misread conjunctions within a single procedure. Failed support produces abstention with candidate evidence for inspection. A production system would need stronger semantic planning and independently checked completeness.

Optional local generation uses FLAN-T5-small, an instruction tuned sequence-to-sequence model, and Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, a causal instruction model [4, 8]. Their compact size makes local execution feasible on constrained hardware; it does not imply superior accuracy. The prompt requests a short exact source phrase answering the question or UNKNOWN. Deterministic decoding produces at most 96 new tokens. The exact-source gate accepts only a normalized source substring containing at least two words. This deliberately restrictive experiment tests verifiable extraction through a language model rather than unrestricted explanation.

For multiple selected policies, the revised generator processes the corresponding question facets separately and requires a verified phrase for every selected source. Accepted wording is restored to the original source capitalization and spacing before presentation. Failure for any selected source produces abstention while leaving complete evidence available for review. This prevents one successfully copied phrase from being mistaken for completion of a two-policy request. It still cannot establish that a short phrase communicates every relevant qualification. The deterministic evidence route therefore provides the most complete policy context, while the model route tests whether concise generation can meet a deliberately conservative output contract.

Fine-tuning was rejected because the small authored benchmark would encourage memorization and does not establish a stable training distribution. A hosted model could improve fluency but adds transfer governance, network dependency and cost. A larger local model or cross-encoder reranker should be considered only after evidence selection and rejection are independently assessed. The implemented stack uses Python, JSONL, Transformers, PyTorch and a local browser server. A broad orchestration framework or managed vector database would add little value at this scale; LangChain remains a scaling option rather than a claimed dependency.

## 4 Experimental protocol results and limitations

Evaluation separates retrieval, answerability and attribution. Macro Recall@k averages the proportion of gold policies retrieved for each supported question. Hit@k asks whether any gold policy appears, while Complete@k requires every gold policy. These differ when a question needs two documents. MRR@3 rewards the first relevant rank. Unsupported questions have no retrieval target and are excluded from these positive-query denominators. Stable policy identifiers prevent duplicate evidence from being counted as distinct relevant discoveries.

Answerability evaluation records acceptance of supported questions and rejection of unsupported questions. Both rates must be reported because refusing everything can produce attractive attack-blocking results. Overall accuracy alone is also misleading when supported cases greatly outnumber unsupported ones. Citation membership checks whether a cited policy appears in the gold set; citation coverage measures how many required sources are cited. Verbatim support verifies textual membership, not relevance, completeness or meaning. Ragas similarly motivates separating retrieval and faithful use of context from general generation quality [6].

Retrieval metrics use the original whole-query top-three ranking, not the facet-expanded response evidence. Complete evidence instead requires an accepted response to cite every annotated policy. This distinction isolates retrieval quality from the downstream planning and evidence-assembly changes. It also avoids calling a refused question complete merely because its candidate list contains the right documents.

In the original protocol, refusal thresholds were selected on development questions and written with corpus and benchmark hashes before test inference. Existing results and the original engine are preserved. Seeded query bootstrap intervals describe sampling variability within the authored set, not population-wide confidence. Version 2 repeats development-only threshold selection after implementation freeze. Its previously observed test results are explicitly regression measurements. The separately frozen audit provides an additional diagnostic check, with limitations from shared project authorship and small subgroups.

Version 1 BM25 achieved Recall@1 of 0.8016, Recall@3 of 0.8968 and MRR@3 of 0.8598 on 63 supported test questions. Dense retrieval improved these values to 0.8810, 0.9841 and 0.9392; neural hybrid reached Recall@3 of 0.9762. However, BM25 falsely accepted four of six unsupported questions and refused 18 supported questions. Dense retrieval still falsely accepted two and refused 20. These results establish the design problem: evidence recall can improve while the decision to answer remains inadequate.

![Figure 2 Version 1 evidence recall and answerability on the original test set](docs/figures/retrieval_results.png)

The original 69-case generation experiments held BM25 and its frozen 0.35 threshold constant. Both models attempted generation on 49 cases. FLAN-T5 returned 28 accepted outputs, with 26 citing gold policies; Qwen returned nine, all citing gold policies. Coverage was 40.58% and 13.04% respectively. These are acceptance and source-membership findings, not semantic accuracy. Strict substring verification can reject useful paraphrases. Different CPU and GPU execution, together with recorded scheduling anomalies, prevent a fair speed comparison from those runs.

Version 2 development calibration selects thresholds of 0.35 for lexical modes and 0.425 for neural cosine. On the previously inspected regression set, dense retrieval accepts 51 of 63 supported questions, falsely accepts one of six unsupported questions and supplies complete gold sources for 48 of 63 supported questions. Corresponding Version 1 counts were 43, two and 40. Answerability accuracy rises from 47/69 to 56/69. This demonstrates repair on known cases, not a validated increase in generalization.

The new audit remains difficult. Dense answerability accuracy is 25/40, or 0.625, compared with 24/40 for its preserved Version 1 baseline. Complete evidence remains 13/28 for both versions. Dense Recall@3 is 0.8750, below BM25's 0.8929, reversing the original retrieval ordering on this harder wording. The gate still accepts five unsupported requests and refuses ten supported ones. Thus the revised architecture improves several known defects without establishing reliable assistance on new questions. Its more permissive semantic coverage also creates a practical tradeoff: higher supported acceptance can accompany more unsupported acceptance. Selecting a deployment mode requires application-specific error costs and additional data, not choosing the highest recall in one experiment.

The Version 2 model audit holds dense retrieval and its frozen threshold constant across all 40 questions. Each model attempts generation on 23 cases. Qwen accepts two responses, both with complete gold sources; FLAN accepts three, only one with complete gold sources. Their overall coverage is 5.0% and 7.5%, and complete evidence occurs for two and one of 28 supported questions respectively. Neither accepts an unsupported audit request, but this comes with 26 and 25 false refusals among supported questions. Zero observed false acceptance in twelve negatives is therefore not evidence of a practically reliable service. The generative adapters demonstrate actual local execution and conservative verification, while the evidence-first extractive route retains greater utility for this prototype.

A concrete failure demonstrates why copy verification is insufficient. For Q002, a course-registration paraphrase ranked a room-booking rule first. FLAN returned “five working days” from that unrelated policy. The output passed exact-source verification but answered the wrong question. The regression target is therefore appropriate source selection and supported completeness, not merely fewer fabricated words. Returning an entire source paragraph can prevent omitted conditions, but it cannot repair retrieval of the wrong policy.

The new audit is not a user study and does not contain actual poisoned source documents, authentic student slang or institutional policy conflicts. Multilingual and adversarial probes are diagnostic cases, not representative subgroup estimates. Topic-term matching can detect regressions but cannot distinguish a correct statement from one that reverses a condition. Before deployment, two reviewers should independently rate factual correctness, required conditions, source entailment and usefulness, resolving disagreements and reporting agreement. Any further implementation changes motivated by audit failures require another untouched sample before claims about unseen performance.

The error analysis also determines how components should be compared. Retrieval ablations hold the response builder fixed; generator comparisons hold retrieved evidence and thresholds fixed. Otherwise a gain attributed to a model might actually come from changed document selection. A useful future ablation would independently remove facet decomposition and whole-paragraph extraction, measuring complete gold-source coverage alongside false acceptance. Development criteria should penalize an unsupported critical answer more heavily than a harmless refusal, but such a cost ratio requires institutional agreement. No arbitrary financial benefit or production acceptance threshold is inferred from the synthetic benchmark.

## 5 Responsible operation and threat mitigation

The principal harm is misplaced authority. A polished answer can cause a student to miss a deadline or assume an entitlement without staff review. The interface identifies the fictional demonstration and exposes policy evidence. A real service needs named policy owners, a dispute route, escalation and incident correction. Eligibility, appeals and individual exceptions remain human decisions. Disclaimers cannot substitute for a workflow that makes verification easy, especially when an answer concerns withdrawal, payments or academic progression.

Data minimization is appropriate because general procedural assistance does not need a transcript, diagnosis or identification number. Basic patterns redact common emails, telephone numbers and student identifiers. Version 2 also screens English requests for credentials and private records while distinguishing public recovery procedures from secret disclosure. These rules can miss unusual wording or incorrectly refuse benign questions. No confidential student database exists in the prototype, and returning a public excerpt to a private-record request must be counted as a scope failure rather than an actual data leak. Future integrations require authorization checks outside the model.

Prompt injection attempts to make user input or retrieved text control the system. OWASP highlights injection, sensitive-information disclosure, supply-chain risks, improper output handling and excessive agency among LLM application vulnerabilities [7]. CampusCompass reduces exposure through read-only functionality, bounded input, intent checks, separate instructions and evidence, and escaped presentation. Its generator cannot execute code, change an account or send a message. These restrictions reduce consequences without proving resistance to attack. A blacklist is incomplete, and secrets should never be supplied to a model on the assumption that a prompt will protect them.

Indirect injection additionally requires controlled ingestion. Real deployment should restrict publishers, retain source hashes, review changes and treat retrieved prose solely as evidence. Policy text must not grant tools or change permissions. Citation identifiers should resolve to validated metadata and actual excerpts. The prototype's stricter schema and source-membership checks support this boundary, but do not certify publisher integrity or semantic entailment. Resource limits, authentication, dependency patching and operational monitoring are still required before exposing the local server as a public service.

Fairness concerns include unequal retrieval for informal English, Arabic, French and mixed-language questions. An English encoder and a few multilingual probes cannot establish equitable performance. Future work requires suitable multilingual encoders, reviewed translations and separately annotated language groups; translating a fluent answer cannot repair incorrect retrieval. Accessibility requires keyboard operation, clear status announcements and sources reachable by assistive technology. Operational governance also requires minimal redacted logs, explicit retention, restricted administration and removal of superseded policies. These controls should be tested with stakeholders rather than inferred from interface appearance.

## 6 Practical value and next steps

The final package includes the executable interface, original policy data, reproducible benchmarks, preserved experiments, source code and this report. Its useful output is a supported procedural response accompanied by evidence, or an explicit abstention when support is inadequate. The decision trace helps explain whether a failure arose from scope handling, retrieval, relevance assessment or response construction. This makes the prototype useful for demonstration and technical review even where its accuracy remains insufficient for autonomous student guidance.

Expected benefits are faster access to approved information and less effort auditing routine answers. Neither reduced staff workload nor improved student satisfaction has been measured. A pilot should compare verified task completion and time to correct information against ordinary website search, accounting for escalation quality and document maintenance. Costs include review effort, model storage, inference, updates and incident handling. Local inference reduces dependence on an external generation endpoint, but does not remove maintenance or privacy obligations.

The immediate priority is independent semantic evaluation on approved real policies, followed by better relevance verification and multiple-question planning where failures justify them. A limited supervised pilot should begin with low-impact informational topics and retain official evidence prominently. Model size, reranking and multilingual expansion should follow measured needs. CampusCompass contributes a reproducible design and an explicit account of how retrieval, source copying and answer correctness differ; operational trust must be earned through stronger evidence than this controlled prototype provides.
