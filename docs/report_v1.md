## Abstract

CampusCompass addresses a practical information-access problem: students express administrative questions in everyday language, while answers are dispersed across formal policies. The proposed service combines document retrieval with a small local language model, returning a short answer, visible evidence and an appropriate refusal when support is inadequate. The delivered prototype uses a deliberately fictional university corpus, a reproducible question benchmark and a browser interface. It separates retrieval, response generation and safety decisions so that failures remain inspectable. This report explains the problem, data preparation, model choices, evaluation and responsible deployment requirements. Its central design principle is that an understandable refusal is preferable to an unsupported policy claim. The prototype establishes technical feasibility within a controlled corpus; it does not establish accuracy on real university documents, multilingual fairness or operational benefit. These require independently authored evaluation data and a supervised institutional pilot.

## 1. Problem definition and justification

A student asking “Can I still drop this class?” may need a registration rule, a deadline, the consequences for the transcript and an office contact. A conventional website can contain all four facts while requiring the student to know several administrative terms before finding them. Email support adds a human interpretation layer, but repeated routine questions compete with unusual cases requiring staff judgment. CampusCompass targets this gap between a user's language and the language of policy. The intended users are students seeking general procedural guidance and service staff checking the source behind a response. The beneficiary is the student, rather than an automated decision-making process.

This project models a fictional institution so that development can be shared without exposing student records or misrepresenting an actual university's rules. Its policies are illustrative design fixtures. No deadline, entitlement, fee or contact in the demo should be treated as real advice. That choice limits external validity, but gives the assessment a coherent environment in which correctness can be checked against known evidence. The same architecture could later ingest approved institutional sources after ownership, access and review arrangements are established.

Natural language processing is justified because queries vary in vocabulary and structure. “Lost access to my account” and “password reset” can concern the same procedure; “withdraw from university” and “withdraw from a course” must remain distinct. The system must retrieve relevant passages, preserve qualifications and communicate the result clearly. A classifier alone can route a question to an office but cannot explain a rule. A keyword search can expose sources but still leaves synthesis to the student. A general chatbot can write a plausible answer while lacking the current local policy. The appropriate solution therefore combines retrieval, constrained synthesis and explicit escalation.

Retrieval-augmented generation supplies external evidence to a generator rather than expecting policy knowledge to reside in model parameters. Lewis et al. motivate this separation through the difficulty of updating and attributing knowledge in parametric models [1]. CampusCompass applies that principle without claiming to reproduce their jointly trained research architecture. Its retrieval and generation components are independently replaceable. Policy updates can change the corpus and index without retraining the language model, while the interface can show the evidence used.

Success has three distinct meanings. Technical success means retrieving the correct policy and generating a supported response. Safety success means refusing unsupported or private requests without preventing ordinary help. Service success means students resolve routine questions more easily. Only the first two can be investigated with the supplied controlled benchmark. Reduced workload, improved satisfaction and better access are expected benefits, not measured outcomes. A production pilot would compare task completion, time to verified answer and escalation quality against the existing search-and-email process.

## 2. Input data and preparation

The input comprises 30 original synthetic policy documents covering common academic and campus service tasks. Each record carries an identifier, title, department, version, effective date and text. The question benchmark contains 106 records, partitioned into 37 development and 69 test cases. Questions and reference annotations are stored separately from policy content. The generator receives retrieved policy text rather than benchmark answers. This separation is essential: indexing expected answers would turn evaluation into answer lookup and inflate apparent performance.

The corpus is intentionally compact enough for a reviewer to inspect. It includes answerable procedures, neighboring policies that can be confused, and questions requiring refusal or clarification. It contains no genuine student names, grades, financial records or medical information. A data card documents authorship, fictional status, permitted use and limitations. A realistic extension would collect approved handbooks, service pages and policy notices, preserving their publisher, revision date, effective interval and original URL. Personal support tickets would require a separate lawful-purpose and access assessment; they are unnecessary for the initial use case.

Ingestion loads JSONL records and rejects an empty corpus or duplicate identifiers. The lexical representation lowercases alphanumeric tokens, removes a documented stop list and maps selected inflections and campus synonyms. Displayed evidence preserves the original capitalization, dates, numerical thresholds and punctuation. This limited normalization supports retrieval; it is not a semantic equivalence test. For real-source ingestion, Unicode and whitespace normalization should retain an untouched source copy, and schema validation should be expanded. Tables need structure-aware extraction, since a fee, deadline and student category can change meaning if their row association disappears. Scanned documents require OCR quality checks before indexing.

The implemented retrieval unit is a complete self-contained policy; the prototype does not split these short fixtures into chunks. Longer real documents should be split along headings and paragraph boundaries, with limited overlap where a condition crosses a boundary. Every chunk must retain its parent document, section, effective date and stable identifier. Chunk length is a tradeoff: very small fragments lose exceptions; very large fragments dilute retrieval and consume generation context. A future development experiment should compare approximately 150, 250 and 400-token units against policy-level relevance labels, measuring recall alongside answer support. These are proposed experimental settings, not completed tuning results.

For the baseline, lexical retrieval uses BM25, whose term-frequency and document-length treatment follows the Okapi family of probabilistic ranking methods [2]. The implementation fixes k1 at 1.5 and b at 0.75, duplicates title text for weighting, and retrieves three policies by default. These are engineering settings, not trained parameters. BM25 suits exact administrative terms and needs no embedding download. Its weakness is lexical mismatch. A documented vocabulary map can improve common paraphrases, but each mapping becomes a maintained assumption and should be tested for unintended matches. A broad synonym such as “leave” can connect unrelated leave-of-absence and room-departure policies.

The optional semantic route uses sentence embeddings. The all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model maps text into 384-dimensional vectors and is intended for similarity and semantic search; its model card identifies English and describes truncation beyond its configured input length [3]. Normalized vectors allow cosine similarity through a dot product. At this scale, exhaustive comparison is simpler than a vector database and makes ranking reproducible. Dense retrieval can recover paraphrases, but similarity does not establish policy applicability. Exact deadlines and negated conditions still require source inspection. Implemented hybrid retrieval sums reciprocal ranks, 1/(60 + rank), across BM25 and dense rankings; lexical fusion substitutes TF-IDF. This avoids assuming incompatible raw scores share a scale.

## 3. Architecture and model selection

![Figure 1 Implemented request processing and evidence controls](docs/figures/architecture.png)

The application follows a deliberately inspectable sequence: validate the request, apply safety and scope checks, retrieve candidate evidence, assess whether a useful response is supported, generate or extract an answer, and return answer plus sources. The browser interface exposes the selected backend and evidence so that a demonstration can distinguish actual model output from a deterministic response. Retrieval results and final response status are separate fields. A response can therefore be evaluated as “correct source retrieved, answer withheld” rather than being compressed into one accuracy score.

An evidence gate prevents generation when the system cannot identify suitable support. The gate is a heuristic operating point, not a calibrated probability of correctness. A high retrieval score may reflect shared words in an irrelevant question, while a low score can hide a valid paraphrase. The safer response to absent evidence is a concise statement that the corpus cannot support an answer, followed by a relevant office or a request for clarification when that information is available. The system must never invent a contact to make a refusal appear helpful.

The implemented local generation adapter supports FLAN-T5-small and Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct through Hugging Face Transformers. The former is an instruction-tuned sequence-to-sequence language model, while the latter provides a causal instruction model and chat template [4, 8]. Small models make a local student demonstration practical; their size is not evidence of superior accuracy. The prompt requests the shortest exact source phrase answering the question, or UNKNOWN. Deterministic decoding bounds the output to 96 new tokens. Generated text is accepted only if it contains at least two words and its normalized form occurs in the selected source. This restrictive gate reduces unsupported paraphrasing but cannot establish relevance, completeness or semantic correctness.

The generator is not permitted to make decisions about eligibility, approve a request, change an account or send a message. It explains general procedures. Source identifiers supplied with evidence support attribution, but a syntactically valid citation is not proof that the cited text entails the answer. The application should validate identifiers against retrieved records and display the original passage. Numeric and deadline claims deserve particular scrutiny because an otherwise fluent paragraph can alter one critical value. Source text remains available even when generated wording is unhelpful.

A deterministic evidence response provides a useful baseline and an availability fallback. It surfaces relevant excerpts without implying that a language model ran. This matters both academically and operationally: the demonstration should still function when model weights are unavailable, and evaluation should reveal whether generation improves on readable retrieval. The project treats a model-download failure as a visible backend condition rather than silently presenting template output as generated text. The final demonstration records the selected mode and the actual execution evidence separately.

Several alternatives were considered. A hosted general-purpose LLM could improve language quality but introduces network dependency, data-transfer governance and variable cost. A larger local instruction model could improve reasoning but requires more memory and slower inference. Fine-tuning on the fictional answers risks memorizing a tiny dataset and is unnecessary for updating policy facts. Supervised fine-tuning becomes defensible only after sufficient de-identified examples show a stable formatting or instruction-following problem that retrieval and prompting cannot resolve. Dense Passage Retrieval demonstrates a learned dual-encoder approach to question–passage retrieval [5]; CampusCompass uses a pretrained embedding option rather than training such a retriever on inadequate local data.

The implementation uses a small Python core, JSON data, a browser interface and explicit command-line scripts. Transformers and PyTorch supply local inference and optional embedding computation, including masked mean pooling and vector normalization. Unit and integration checks target meaningful boundaries such as missing evidence, source association and safety handling. A large orchestration framework would add dependency and debugging overhead without a corresponding benefit for this pipeline. LangChain, a managed vector store and a cross-encoder reranker remain reasonable scaling options, but are not prerequisites for a sound design or claimed components of the minimal prototype.

## 4. Evaluation design and interpretation

The benchmark distinguishes ordinary answerable questions, paraphrases, questions outside the corpus, multiple-policy questions, privacy-related requests and prompt-injection attempts. It includes 90 single-policy positives across both splits, 12 unanswerables and four additional multi-policy or privacy-related cases. Each answerable item identifies relevant document IDs and diagnostic topic terms. Development and test labels are explicit. Threshold selection uses only development cases, with the chosen settings saved before test predictions. Because the corpus and benchmark share a synthetic authoring process, this fixed split is not blind or independent validation and cannot eliminate stylistic leakage. Independent questions and genuine documents are needed before making deployment claims.

Retrieval evaluation asks whether the correct evidence reaches the generator. Recall@k is the proportion of relevant documents retrieved among all relevant documents for an answerable question, averaged across such questions. Hit@k instead asks whether at least one relevant document appears; the two differ when multiple policies are required. Mean reciprocal rank emphasizes the position of the first relevant result. These metrics should use stable document IDs so that overlapping chunks do not count as separate discoveries. Non-answerable cases should be excluded from positive retrieval averages and assessed through rejection behavior.

Answer evaluation asks a different question: does the response communicate the required supported facts? The supplied automated checks examine expected facts or terms and the expected status. They are regression indicators, not full semantic correctness judgments. A response may include a required phrase while reversing its meaning, or paraphrase correctly without matching it. A stronger review rubric rates factual correctness, coverage of conditions, source entailment and usefulness separately. Two reviewers should judge a sample independently and resolve disagreements, particularly for deadline, fee and exception cases.

Citation evaluation also needs care. Citation presence measures whether a response includes sources. Citation validity measures whether those identifiers exist and were retrieved. Citation support requires checking whether the source actually supports each material claim. Only the last directly addresses groundedness. Ragas motivates evaluating retrieval quality, faithful use of context and generation quality as separate dimensions [6]. An LLM judge could assist a larger pilot, but its score should be audited against human annotations and should not become the sole authority for policy correctness.

Safety evaluation records refusal on privacy and injection cases, and false refusal on legitimate questions. Reporting only attack blocking would reward a system that rejects everything. Unknown-question rejection must likewise be balanced against answerable-question coverage. Latency should be measured separately for retrieval and generation, distinguishing first model load from warm requests. The report does not translate a small number of timings into a service-level guarantee. Hardware, backend, model revision and generation limit must accompany any performance number.

The evaluation scripts produce machine-readable per-question results and a summary so a reviewer can reproduce aggregate values and inspect failures. The benchmark is a diagnostic demonstration, not an estimate of real student demand or a statistically representative accuracy study. Comparisons should run lexical retrieval, the selected retrieval configuration and optional dense/hybrid modes under the same cases. A generator comparison should hold retrieved context constant to isolate generation quality. Conversely, retrieval ablations should use the same response builder. This prevents attributing improvements to one component when several changed simultaneously.

The completed lexical experiment selects an overlap threshold of 0.35 using development data. On 63 answerable test cases, BM25 achieves macro Recall@1 of 0.8016, Recall@3 of 0.8968 and MRR@3 of 0.8598. TF-IDF reaches Recall@3 of 0.9206 and lexical rank fusion 0.9127. The small differences do not establish general superiority. BM25's query-bootstrap 95% interval for Recall@3 is approximately 0.8175–0.9603, and shared authorship further limits interpretation. All three lexical modes accept 45 of 63 answerable cases while rejecting only two of six unanswerables: 18 false abstentions and four false accepts. Their answerability accuracy is 47/69, or 0.6812. This is inadequate for unsupervised policy guidance.

The measured neural comparison uses development-selected thresholds of 0.30:

| Retriever | Recall at 1 | Recall at 3 | MRR at 3 | False accepts | False refusals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dense MiniLM | 0.8810 | 0.9841 | 0.9392 | 2 of 6 | 20 of 63 |
| BM25 plus dense | 0.8651 | 0.9762 | 0.9339 | 4 of 6 | 19 of 63 |

Dense improves evidence retrieval on this authored sample, but higher recall does not automatically improve the decision to answer. Rank fusion provides no demonstrated advantage here. The appropriate next experiment is an independently evaluated relevance or answerability gate, rather than assuming hybrid complexity guarantees better results.

Full 69-case local-model experiments use BM25 and its frozen threshold of 0.35. Both adapters attempt generation on 49 cases. FLAN-T5 accepts 28 responses, of which 26 cite gold policies; Qwen accepts nine, all citing gold policies. Overall response coverage is therefore 40.58% and 13.04% respectively. These are acceptance and citation-membership results, not answer-accuracy scores. Qwen's many refusals reflect the strict exact-phrase gate and do not establish that its unrestricted answers would be wrong. CPU FLAN and GPU Qwen timings are not fair speed comparisons; earlier 12-case smoke runs are preserved separately.

The failure trace explains why source copying is insufficient. For Q002, a course-registration question retrieves room-booking policy first, and FLAN returns “five working days” from that unrelated source. The phrase is copied accurately but answers the wrong question. Baseline sentence-copy support is 1.0 by construction and must not be presented as perfect correctness. Multiple-policy questions expose the single-source response builder: retrieval can find both documents while the answer cites only one. Explicit injection examples occur in development; separate post-test challenge diagnostics do not establish independently validated robustness. Privacy false accepts expose public synthetic excerpts, not confidential records. Improving rejection, relevance verification and evidence coverage takes priority over fluency.

Release decisions should depend on failure severity as well as averages. A missed library procedure and a fabricated withdrawal deadline should not carry identical operational weight. A supervised pilot should begin with low-impact informational topics, display official evidence prominently and offer human escalation. Proposed acceptance criteria include no unsupported critical claims in a reviewed acceptance set, consistently valid citations, and explicit handling of unanswerable cases. These criteria are design requirements awaiting institutional agreement, not achieved production certifications.

## 5. Ethics, security and responsible operation

The main risk is misplaced authority. Students may interpret a polished answer as an official determination and miss a deadline or disclose unnecessary information. The interface therefore identifies the fictional demonstration, shows source evidence and frames answers as procedural assistance. A real service would need named policy owners, a route to dispute an answer and an incident process for correcting harmful guidance. Human staff retain responsibility for exceptions, appeals and individual eligibility decisions. A disclaimer alone is insufficient; the workflow must make verification and escalation practical.

Privacy begins with data minimization. General policy assistance does not need a student's identification number, transcript or diagnosis. The demo uses synthetic content and basic checks for common sensitive patterns. Pattern matching is incomplete: it can miss names and unusual identifiers and can flag benign text. It should not be described as comprehensive anonymization. A deployment should avoid storing raw chat by default, redact necessary operational logs, restrict administrator access and publish a retention schedule. Any student-record integration requires authorization checks outside the model, since a prompt cannot enforce access control.

Prompt injection is a distinct threat in which user input or retrieved content attempts to redirect the model. OWASP identifies injection, sensitive information disclosure, supply-chain issues, improper output handling and excessive agency among its LLM application risks [7]. CampusCompass reduces exposure through bounded read-only functionality, separate instructions and evidence, simple suspicious-input checks and visible refusals. These controls are partial. A malicious passage can look like legitimate prose, and wording variants can evade a blacklist. The model must never receive secrets merely because the system prompt says not to reveal them.

Indirect injection requires protecting ingestion as well as chat. A production index should accept only approved publishers, preserve document hashes and require review for policy changes. Retrieved text is untrusted evidence, never an instruction source. The application must not execute generated code, follow generated links automatically or grant tools based on a passage. Output should be rendered as escaped text or sanitized Markdown. Source URLs should come from validated metadata rather than unrestricted model invention. Resource limits and request throttling would reduce accidental or deliberate consumption; the local assessment prototype is not a hardened public endpoint.

Fairness concerns arise even without demographic data. Students unfamiliar with formal English may phrase questions differently and experience worse retrieval. Optional English embeddings do not establish support for Arabic, French or mixed-language queries. The implemented evaluation should therefore be described within its tested language boundary. Future multilingual work requires appropriate multilingual embeddings, separately reviewed translations and subgroup error analysis with consent. Translating an answer cannot repair retrieval of the wrong policy. Accessibility also requires keyboard operation, clear status messages, readable contrast and evidence that screen-reader users can reach the sources.

Policy currency is another ethical responsibility. An accurate answer from an obsolete rule can still be harmful. Institutional ingestion should track effective dates, invalidate superseded chunks and surface uncertainty when records conflict. The initial corpus is a static educational snapshot, so automated freshness monitoring is proposed. A model or dependency update should trigger regression evaluation and a rollback option. Model-card limitations, licenses and exact revisions belong in the repository. These governance tasks are part of maintaining a trustworthy service, rather than optional documentation after deployment.

## 6. Expected outputs, value and development roadmap

The deliverable is a complete demonstration package: executable application, readable policy corpus, annotated questions, evaluation script, technical report and reproduction instructions. A successful response provides a concise answer, relevant evidence and source identifiers; an unsuccessful request produces a meaningful status and an escalation or clarification route where supported. The browser demonstration should show a routine policy query, a paraphrase, an unsupported question and an adversarial request, making both capability and limitations visible.

The expected value comes from reducing the effort needed to locate and understand approved information. Source-linked answers may help staff audit guidance and identify missing documentation. These benefits remain hypotheses until a user study measures completion time, verified correctness and satisfaction. Costs include document maintenance, review effort, compute, model storage and incidents. Local inference reduces dependence on an external generation endpoint, but it does not remove security obligations or make a small model reliable by default.

The next stage should prioritize independent evaluation and better evidence selection before increasing model complexity. First, replace fictional fixtures with a narrowly approved public-policy subset and establish ownership. Second, collect independently written questions and annotate supporting passages. Third, compare chunking, lexical, dense and hybrid retrieval on development data, freeze the configuration and assess the held-out set. Finally, run a supervised pilot with clear escalation and documented error review. CampusCompass offers a defensible NLP solution because its architecture makes evidence, uncertainty and responsibility inspectable, while its claims remain proportional to what a controlled prototype can demonstrate.
